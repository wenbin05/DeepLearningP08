\section{Abstract}
We investigate whether LoRA placement in BART-base's encoder self-attention,
decoder self-attention, or encoder--decoder cross-attention affects summarization
quality under matched trainable-parameter budgets. Each region adapts query and
value projections at rank eight, yielding exactly 147,456 trainable parameters.
We completed a single-seed pilot on a fixed 1,024-example CNN/DailyMail training
subset for 32 optimizer updates and evaluated 64 held-out validation examples.
Encoder, decoder, and cross-attention LoRA achieved ROUGE-1 scores of 41.02,
41.32, and 41.31, respectively, compared with 41.04 for unadapted BART-base.
All three LoRA variants reduced validation loss, but generated-summary ROUGE
changes were small and did not consistently improve across metrics. These
preliminary measurements establish an operational, capacity-controlled comparison;
they do not establish a reliable ranking of placements. Additional seeds,
full fine-tuning and PEFT baselines, full test-set and cross-domain evaluation,
and human factual-consistency assessment remain outstanding.

\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
Abstractive summarization compresses lengthy source documents into fluent, salient, and factually accurate summaries. Pre-trained sequence-to-sequence Transformers such as BART~\cite{lewis2020bart} achieve high ROUGE scores by leveraging bidirectional encoder representations coupled with autoregressive decoder generation. However, conventional adaptation requires updating all 139.4 million parameters in BART-base, incurring steep computational overhead and high storage requirements when maintaining separate checkpoints for multiple domains or tasks. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, particularly Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{hu2022lora}, circumvent this cost by freezing the pre-trained weights $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ and injecting trainable rank-decomposition matrices $\Delta W = B A$.

\textbf{Midterm Scope and Rubric Alignment.} This midterm presents zero-shot
BART-base and the three isolated LoRA placements in a bounded pilot. The supplied
P08 guide also requests full fine-tuning, Prefix Tuning, rank/projection ablations,
cross-domain evaluation, and a human study. These requirements are not treated as
waived. Their implementation or preparation is distinguished below from completed
experiments; the pilot does not constitute published-baseline reproduction.

\textbf{Research Question.} Under strictly matched trainable parameter budgets and identical training conditions, how does the architectural placement of LoRA adapters (encoder self-attention vs. decoder self-attention vs. encoder-decoder cross-attention) govern summarization performance, factual consistency, and zero-shot cross-domain generalization?

\section{Related Work (Updated)}
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning for conditional text generation has evolved rapidly, transitioning from uniform parameter perturbation to structural and capacity-aware allocation.

\textbf{Parameter-Efficient Sequence-to-Sequence Modeling.} Large-scale pre-training of sequence-to-sequence architectures, exemplified by BART~\cite{lewis2020bart}, established denoising objectives as the foundation for modern summarization. While full fine-tuning updates all parameters, Prefix-Tuning~\cite{li2021prefix} introduced continuous virtual prefix vectors prepended to attention keys and values, preserving frozen model weights while achieving competitive generation on XSum with a small task-specific parameter budget. Concurrently, LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} demonstrated that task-specific parameter updates exhibit low intrinsic dimensionality, parametrizing weight updates via low-rank matrices $\Delta W = BA$. In their foundational work, Hu et al. observed that adapting attention query and value projections suffices for autoregressive language models. Its benchmarks include both language understanding and generation; our experiment specifically tests architectural regions within BART.

\textbf{Non-Uniform Capacity Allocation and Layer Sensitivity.} Recognizing that uniform low-rank parameterization across all layers may be sub-optimal, recent research has explored dynamic allocation. AdaLoRA~\cite{zhang2023adalora} parameterizes weight matrices using singular value decomposition and iteratively prunes singular values based on importance scores, allocating higher rank to influential matrices. Similarly, WeightLoRA~\cite{veprikov2026weightlora} evaluates adapter significance during fine-tuning, demonstrating that adapters are not uniformly useful throughout BART. In a broader theoretical analysis, He et al.~\cite{he2022unified} established a unified framework connecting adapters, prefix tuning, and LoRA, comparing the design choices shared by several PEFT methods. Furthermore, Valipour et al.~\cite{valipour2023dylora} introduced DyLoRA to train models across a spectrum of ranks dynamically, supporting selection among multiple ranks without separately training every rank. These approaches motivate capacity-aware adaptation. Our study instead compares distinct, interpretable architectural regions under an identical, static adapter budget.

\textbf{Factual Consistency in Abstractive Summarization.} A known limitation of n-gram overlap metrics such as ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L is their insensitivity to factual hallucinations. Kryscinski et al.~\cite{kryscinski2020evaluating} demonstrated that high-ROUGE summaries frequently introduce factual inconsistencies unsupported by the source text, proposing FactCC as a weakly supervised evaluation metric. Automatic factuality assessment also has domain limitations. We plan to complement ROUGE with a human study; neither a FactCC experiment nor human judgments are available in this repository.

\section{Methodology}
Our methodology enforces strict experimental consistency to reduce confounding when assessing whether performance differences originate from adapter placement rather than training variations or capacity disparities.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Placement Isolation}
We use pre-trained \texttt{facebook/bart-base} as our backbone, which comprises an encoder with 6 Transformer layers and a decoder with 6 Transformer layers ($d_{\text{model}} = 768$, 12 attention heads, feed-forward dimension $d_{\text{ff}} = 3072$, totaling 139,420,416 parameters). For any weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, LoRA parameterizes the forward pass as:
\begin{equation}
h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A x
\end{equation}
where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ uses PEFT's default Kaiming-uniform initialization, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ is initialized to zero, $r$ is the adaptation rank, and $\alpha$ is a scaling hyperparameter. We set $r = 8$ and $\alpha = 16$, targeting the query ($W_q$) and value ($W_v$) projections ($k = d = 768$). Each adapted projection introduces $2 \times 768 \times 8 = 12,288$ parameters.

We implement four LoRA configurations; the three isolated placements were executed in this pilot, while all-attention LoRA remains a comparison to run:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Standard LoRA (All Attention):} Injects adapters into $W_q$ and $W_v$ across all 6 encoder self-attention, 6 decoder self-attention, and 6 decoder cross-attention blocks (18 attention layers total $\times 24,576 = 442,368$ trainable parameters, $0.32\%$ of the base model).
  \item \textbf{Encoder Self-Attention LoRA:} Adapts only \texttt{model.encoder.layers.*.self\_attn} ($6 \times 24,576 = 147,456$ trainable parameters, $0.11\%$).
  \item \textbf{Decoder Self-Attention LoRA:} Adapts only \texttt{model.decoder.layers.*.self\_attn} ($6 \times 24,576 = 147,456$ trainable parameters, $0.11\%$).
  \item \textbf{Cross-Attention LoRA:} Adapts only \texttt{model.decoder.layers.*.encoder\_attn} ($6 \times 24,576 = 147,456$ trainable parameters, $0.11\%$).
\end{enumerate}
Crucially, configurations (2), (3), and (4) share an identical parameter budget of exactly 147,456 parameters, controlling adapter capacity in the architectural comparison.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}
CNN/DailyMail version 3.0.0 has 287,113 training, 13,368 validation, and
11,490 test examples. We selected 1,024 training and 64 validation examples
by shuffling each official split with data seed 42. The identical subsets are
used across placement runs; zero-shot evaluation uses the same validation subset.
The training and validation example-ID hashes in the exported manifests match
across runs and were independently reproduced by reloading the pinned dataset.
No test results are included in the evidence supplied for this report.

Sources truncate to 1,024 BPE tokens and training targets to 128 tokens.
The tokenizer handles special tokens; dynamic label padding is masked with
$-100$ in the loss. ROUGE uses the original, untruncated reference summaries.
We use the official Hugging Face Evaluate ROUGE implementation, with stemming,
macro mean per-example F1 multiplied by 100, and ROUGE-L rather than ROUGE-Lsum.
Validation loss uses truncated teacher-forced targets, so its improvement does
not imply an improvement in generated-summary ROUGE or factual consistency.

The recorded model revision is
\nolinkurl{aadd2ab0ae0c8268c7c9693540e9904811f36177}; the CNN/DailyMail revision is
\nolinkurl{96df5e686bee6baa90b8bee7c28b81fa3fa6223d}. All runs record clean code
revision \texttt{6c10d0a}, the same pinned metric revision, generation settings,
and package environment. The final checkpoint is selected after the fixed update
budget, without test-set selection. Full configurations and raw exported pilot
records accompany the repository in \texttt{experiments/midterm\_pilot\_seed42}.

For future transfer evaluation, the code loads the same CNN/DailyMail-trained
checkpoint on XSum without optimization. XSum has 204,045 training, 11,332
validation, and 11,334 test examples. No XSum metrics were supplied. Applying
the current minimum generation length of 56 to single-sentence XSum summaries
is a protocol choice to examine explicitly in subsequent evaluation.

\section{Preliminary Results}
\subsection{Measured Budget-Matched Placement Comparison}
The supplied Colab export contains four completed validation records:
unadapted BART-base and the three isolated LoRA placements. Saved Trainer
statistics are consistent with one epoch and 32 updates for each adapted model.
Each placement targets 12 projections across six blocks and records exactly
147,456 trainable parameters. This is 0.1058\% of the base-model parameter
count, or 0.10565\% of total parameters including adapters. Zero-shot inference
has zero trainable parameters.

\subsection{Observed Differences and Interpretation}
Decoder self-attention has the highest observed ROUGE-1 (41.3206) and
ROUGE-L (25.9262) in this sample; cross-attention has the lowest validation
loss (3.2401). The decoder--cross ROUGE-1 difference is only 0.0094 points.
The entire ROUGE-1 spread among the three placements is approximately 0.30
points. Relative to zero-shot ROUGE-1 (41.0442), decoder and cross-attention
increase by approximately 0.28 and 0.27 points, while encoder-only adaptation
changes by approximately $-0.02$ points.

The metric changes are mixed. Zero-shot ROUGE-L is 25.8363: decoder-only
adaptation increases it by about 0.09 points, while cross-attention and encoder-only
adaptation decrease it by about 0.11 and 0.36 points. All three adapted models
reduce teacher-forced validation loss from the baseline's 3.6372 to approximately
3.24--3.27. The distinction between lower loss and mixed ROUGE changes reinforces
the need to evaluate actual generated text rather than relying on loss alone.

With one seed, 64 validation examples, and short training, these observations do
not identify a statistically reliable best placement or establish convergence.
No standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance claims are reported.
The pilot does not support a claim that cross-attention consistently outperforms
decoder or encoder adaptation, nor a claim about factuality or transfer.

\subsection{Timing and Evidence Boundaries}
Recorded training-loop wall times are 135.4 seconds for encoder self-attention,
105.5 seconds for decoder self-attention, and 103.7 seconds for cross-attention.
These measurements include the Trainer's epoch validation and checkpoint saving;
they exclude initial model/data preparation and the separately timed final
validation prediction pass. The latter takes approximately 50.5--57.2 seconds
for the LoRA runs. Zero-shot's zero training time means no optimization was
performed, not that inference was free: its validation took 67.5 seconds.
These single-run times do not establish a robust efficiency ranking or equal
wall-clock/FLOP budgets.

The raw uploaded JSON and Markdown table agree at their reported precision.
Configuration, target-module, parameter-count, environment, data-hash, and metric
checks establish internal consistency of the supplied records. Predictions and
checkpoints were not uploaded, so ROUGE values were not independently recomputed.
The exported aggregate scores provide no per-example factuality assessment.

\section{Challenges and Adaptations}
The original training scaffold did not connect ROUGE to the Trainer, and its
fallback evaluator substituted invalid approximate scores. We replaced this
with pinned official Evaluate ROUGE, explicit failure propagation, and saved
configuration/metric/prediction artifacts. Shared model construction now checks
actual PEFT targets and trainable counts; one evaluation-only path supports both
test domains. CPU training, generation, and checkpoint-reload smoke tests were
executed; the documented suite passed 29 tests before the pilot.

Colab's newer notebook Python initially conflicted with the pinned experiment
stack. The setup now creates a separate Python 3.12.12 environment. Under the
midterm deadline, we used a bounded single-seed pilot with a 1,024-example training
subset and 64 validation examples, preserving matched placement budgets and
settings. This adaptation supplies genuine preliminary measurements but limits
what can be concluded and does not replace the remaining rubric requirements.


A result-validation script checks the uploaded records, reproduces the report
table and single-seed parameter plot, and rejects incompatible or missing evidence.
Remaining work prioritizes comparison baselines, additional seeds, held-out
transfer, and factuality evaluation, rather than interpreting the current small
ROUGE differences as definitive.
